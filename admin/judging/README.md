# Judging Materials

This folder contains all materials needed to evaluate participant projects in a fair, consistent, and constructive manner. These resources help create a competitive yet supportive environment that motivates participants while ensuring objective assessment.

## Judging/Evaluation
At its base, this is meant o be an educational experience for the students participating. To this end, we modeled our approach to feedback/judging as an accelerated 'mini-thesis' project. This means that students have multiple opportunities to receive feedback from experts in the field throughout the hackathon, aiming to develop and refine their problem statements and proposed solutions.

We suggest holding 2-3 feedback sessions before the final presentation. In these feedback sessions, have students present to a panel (or two) of graduate students (and 1-2 faculty/postdocs). In the first, focus mainly on motivation and setting up the problem, and in the others, help them develop their story and if needed suggest areas that could be explored. None of the students or faculty included in these sessions should be judges at the end of the competition. Here is an example of how these sessions might go:

**Day 1**: After introducing datasets, judging criteria, and forming teams, have teams work on problem statement development for 1-2 hours, reviewing literature and identifying potential gaps/areas of knowledge. Then, have them present a 1-2 slide proposal to 1 or 2 panels of 3-4 grad students.

**End of Day 1, or beginning of Day 2**: Have teams give a brief (5 min) update on their projects to a panel of grad students/faculty including initial results. Try to temper expectations here - many (if not all) groups will be overestimating the amount of analyses they can complete - try pivoting them to a more manageable question. Think of it as a brief 'committee meeting'/proposal.

**End of Day 2/1-2 hours before final presentations**: Review presentation and final results with a panel - if there is time, have them practice, otherwise step through slides quickly. Again, many teams will feel that they can push just a bit more and finalize results - make sure they have backup plans/have a story to tell with the results they have right now.

**Final Presentations**: Bring in 3-5 judges that have not been involved with the hackathon at all up to this point. Have each fill out their rubrics independently, and then convene in a separate room to discuss. Often, we give prizes for overall winners, as well as winners in each category. For any teams that haven't won, we also would give bonus/fun prizes (maybe a team was particularly funny, or stayed latest at night, etc.) Ideal judges are faculty, postdocs, senior-level PhD students, and industry professionals in neuroscience, ML, BCI, or data science. Make sure to send the scoring rubrics out at least a day in advance, and that the judges are familiar with the structure/content of the workshop.

##  Judging Categories

1. **Scientific Merit** (30-40%)
   - Question clarity and significance
   - Approach appropriateness
   - Understanding of methods

2. **Technical Implementation** (30-40%)
   - Code quality and organization
   - Proper use of techniques learned
   - Handling of edge cases

3. **Communication** (20-30%)
   - Presentation clarity
   - Visualization quality
   - Ability to explain results

4. **Creativity/Innovation** (10-20%)
   - Novel approaches
   - Going beyond basic requirements
   - Interesting insights
